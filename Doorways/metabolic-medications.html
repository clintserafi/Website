How metabolic medications work — and why the differences matter

Not all GLP-1 medications are the same. Each drug targets a different combination of metabolic receptors, producing meaningfully different effects in your brain, gut, and fat tissue.

How They Work

Three receptors. Three different sets of levers.

Each receptor type is a distinct signaling pathway. When a medication activates one, it triggers specific downstream effects in your brain, gut, pancreas, liver, and fat tissue. More receptors targeted means more metabolic levers pulled simultaneously.

Receptor 1 — All three medications

GLP-1 — “The Appetite Brake”

Glucagon-Like Peptide-1 receptor
Semaglutide Tirzepatide Retatrutide
Slows your stomach down Food moves through your stomach more slowly, extending fullness after smaller meals.
Turns down hunger at the source Acts directly on the hypothalamus — your brain's hunger control center — reducing appetite signals before they even become conscious cravings.
Triggers "I'm full" signals sooner Activates the brainstem's satiety center, so the message to stop eating arrives earlier in a meal and lasts longer after it.
Stabilizes blood sugar Prompts the pancreas to release insulin when blood sugar rises — and only then, making it safe without risking dangerous blood sugar drops in healthy individuals.
Quiets food cravings and reward-driven eating Influences the brain's dopamine reward pathways, reducing the pull toward high-calorie foods. Many patients also notice a marked decrease in alcohol cravings — same mechanism.
Receptor 2 — Tirzepatide + Retatrutide

GIP — “The Amplifier”

Glucose-Dependent Insulinotropic Polypeptide receptor
Tirzepatide Retatrutide
Amplifies what GLP-1 started Works in synergy with GLP-1 to produce significantly stronger appetite suppression and blood sugar control than either signal alone. It's not additive — it multiplies the effect.
Directs fuel toward muscle Helps route glucose and available energy toward metabolically active muscle tissue rather than fat storage — relevant to body composition, not just the number on the scale.
Additional brain-level satiety support GIP receptors in the brain reinforce the appetite-suppressing signals from GLP-1, contributing to the stronger satiety response seen with dual-agonist medications.
Supports bone health GIP receptors in bone tissue promote bone formation — a benefit independent of its metabolic effects, relevant for adults managing bone density.
Receptor 3 — Retatrutide only

Glucagon — “The Furnace”

Glucagon receptor (GCGR)
Retatrutide only
Raises your resting calorie burn Increases thermogenesis — your body burns more calories simply existing. This is distinct from anything the other two medications do: it adds genuine metabolic expenditure, not just appetite reduction.
Unlocks stored fat to be burned as fuel Signals fat cells to actively release stored fat (lipolysis) so the body can use it for energy — the key mechanism driving deeper fat loss compared to GLP-1 alone.
Targets liver fat directly Directly promotes the breakdown of fat stored in the liver (hepatic steatosis) — a metabolic risk factor that many active adults carry silently, often undetected.
Shifts from intake-reduction to output-increase While GLP-1 reduces what goes in, glucagon agonism increases what gets burned. Together they create a true double-sided metabolic effect — the reason early data shows markedly greater weight loss.
